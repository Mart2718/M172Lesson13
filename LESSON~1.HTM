Why this matters — Bridges, balance, and identities

How can an engineer design a bridge that safely holds weight as it flexes in the wind? Trigonometric identities make it possible. By using double-angle and Pythagorean identities, engineers can simplify the formulas that describe how a bridge's cables and beams move under stress. These identities turn messy expressions into simpler ones — helping engineers predict how forces add, balance, or cancel, and ensuring the bridge stays strong and stable.

Today we do two big things: we solve trig equations and we simplify or verify trig identities. Both depend on the same toolkit — recognizing patterns and rewriting expressions in equivalent forms.

1. Solving Basic Trig Equations

Strategy

To solve an equation like \( \sin(B \cdot x) = c \), find all reference angles on \( [0, 2\pi) \) where \( \sin(\theta) = c \). Then set the inside expression equal to each angle plus the period, and solve for \( x \). Add \( + 2\pi k \) (or \( + \pi k \) for tangent) to capture all solutions.

Common mistake: Forgetting that \( \sin \) and \( \cos \) have two reference angles per cycle. Tangent has one per cycle (period \( \pi \)).

Predict first: How many solutions to \( \sin(3x) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \) lie in \( [0, 2\pi) \)?



Six solutions. Sine takes the value \( -\tfrac{1}{2} \) twice per cycle, and \( y = \sin(3x) \) completes three cycles on \( [0, 2\pi) \). So \(2 \times 3 = 6\) solutions on that interval.

Example 1a: \( \sin(3x) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \)

Try this yourself before revealing the steps.

Solve for the exact solutions.

Step 1: Find all angles \( \theta \) in \( [0, 2\pi) \) where \( \sin(\theta) = -\tfrac{1}{2} \). \[ \theta = \tfrac{7\pi}{6}, \quad \tfrac{11\pi}{6} \]
Step 2: Set the inside \( 3x \) equal to each angle, adding period \(2\pi k\) to capture all solutions. \[ 3x = \tfrac{7\pi}{6} + 2\pi k \quad \text{or} \quad 3x = \tfrac{11\pi}{6} + 2\pi k \]
Step 3: Divide each equation by 3. \[ x = \tfrac{7\pi}{18} + \tfrac{2\pi k}{3} \quad \text{or} \quad x = \tfrac{11\pi}{18} + \tfrac{2\pi k}{3} \]
Answer: \( x = \dfrac{7\pi}{18} + \dfrac{2\pi k}{3} \) or \( x = \dfrac{11\pi}{18} + \dfrac{2\pi k}{3} \), \(k \in \mathbb{Z}\)

Example 1b: \( \tan\!\left(x - \tfrac{\pi}{4}\right) = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} \)

Try this yourself before revealing the steps.

Solve for the exact solutions.

Step 1: Recognize \( \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{3} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \). Find one angle in \( [0, \pi) \) where \( \tan(\theta) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \). \[ \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{6} \] (Tangent has period \( \pi \), so we only need one base angle.)
Step 2: Set inside expression equal to base angle plus \( \pi k \). \[ x - \tfrac{\pi}{4} = \tfrac{\pi}{6} + \pi k \]
Step 3: Solve for \( x \). Common denominator is 12. \[ x = \tfrac{\pi}{6} + \tfrac{\pi}{4} + \pi k = \tfrac{2\pi}{12} + \tfrac{3\pi}{12} + \pi k \]
Answer: \( x = \dfrac{5\pi}{12} + \pi k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} \)

2. Solving with a Calculator and Inverse Functions

When to reach for the calculator

When the right side is not a unit-circle value (like \( -0.8528 \) or \( \tfrac{1}{3} \)), use \( \sin^{-1} \), \( \cos^{-1} \), or \( \tan^{-1} \) on a calculator. Then use symmetry to find the second solution in the cycle.

⚠ Calculator caveat: Inverse functions return only one angle. For sine, the second angle is \( \pi - \theta_1 \). For cosine, the second angle is \( 2\pi - \theta_1 \) (or equivalently \( -\theta_1 \)).

Example 2a: \( \cos(9\theta) = -0.8528 \)

Try this yourself before revealing the steps.

Step 1: Take the inverse cosine to find the first angle. \[ 9\theta_1 = \cos^{-1}(-0.8528) \approx 2.6012 \]
Step 2: Use the cosine symmetry — second angle is \( 2\pi - 2.6012 \). \[ 9\theta_2 \approx 6.2832 - 2.6012 = 3.6820 \]
Step 3: Add the period \( 2\pi k \) and divide by 9. \[ \theta = \tfrac{2.6012 + 2\pi k}{9} \quad \text{or} \quad \theta = \tfrac{3.6820 + 2\pi k}{9} \]
Answer: \( \theta \approx 0.2890 + \tfrac{2\pi k}{9} \) or \( \theta \approx 0.4091 + \tfrac{2\pi k}{9} \)

Example 2b: \( 3\sin(x - 4) = 1 \)

Try this yourself before revealing the steps.

Step 1: Isolate sine. \[ \sin(x - 4) = \tfrac{1}{3} \]
Step 2: Take inverse sine for the first angle. \[ x - 4 = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{1}{3}\right) \approx 0.3398 \]
Step 3: Sine symmetry — second angle is \( \pi - 0.3398 \). \[ x - 4 \approx \pi - 0.3398 \approx 2.8018 \]
Step 4: Add \( 2\pi k \) and add 4 to both sides. \[ x \approx 4.3398 + 2\pi k \quad \text{or} \quad x \approx 6.8018 + 2\pi k \]
Answer: \( x \approx 4.3398 + 2\pi k \) or \( x \approx 6.8018 + 2\pi k \)

3. Application — A Ferris Wheel Ride

Problem

A rider's height (in meters) above the ground is modeled by \[ h(t) = 22 + 18\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{15}t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right), \quad t \text{ in seconds.} \] At what time(s) in the first 60 seconds is the rider at 30 meters?

Predict first: Looking at the graph, roughly how many times in the first 60 seconds does the rider reach 30 m?



Two times. The wheel completes one full revolution in 30 seconds, so the rider passes the 30 m mark twice on the way up/down per revolution — and there are two revolutions in 60 seconds. But because the rider only crosses 30 m at specific moments, we get exactly four crossings (two per revolution × two revolutions). Solve to confirm!

Worked solution

Try setting it up yourself first.

Step 1: Set \( h(t) = 30 \) and isolate sine. \[ 30 = 22 + 18\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{15}t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sin\!\left(\tfrac{\pi}{15}t - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right) = \tfrac{8}{18} = \tfrac{4}{9} \]
Step 2: Take inverse sine. First angle: \[ \tfrac{\pi}{15}t - \tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx \sin^{-1}\!\left(\tfrac{4}{9}\right) \approx 0.4606 \] Second angle (sine symmetry): \[ \tfrac{\pi}{15}t - \tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx \pi - 0.4606 \approx 2.6810 \]
Step 3: Add \( \tfrac{\pi}{2} \approx 1.5708 \) to both sides (and add \( 2\pi k \)). \[ \tfrac{\pi}{15}t \approx 2.0314 + 2\pi k \quad \text{or} \quad \tfrac{\pi}{15}t \approx 4.2518 + 2\pi k \]
Step 4: Multiply both sides by \( \tfrac{15}{\pi} \). \[ t \approx 9.7 + 30k \quad \text{or} \quad t \approx 20.3 + 30k \]
Step 5: Restrict to \( t \in [0, 60] \) seconds. Plug in \( k = 0, 1 \). \[ t \approx 9.7,\; 20.3,\; 39.7,\; 50.3 \text{ seconds} \]
Answer: The rider is at 30 m at approximately \( t \approx 9.7,\, 20.3,\, 39.7, \) and \( 50.3 \) seconds.

4. Simplifying with Fundamental Identities

Definition — Identity

An identity is an equation between two expressions that are always equal. Identities can be used to replace one expression with the other in any context.

Reciprocal Identities

Cosecant
\[ \csc(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{\sin(\theta)} \]
Secant
\[ \sec(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{\cos(\theta)} \]
Cotangent
\[ \cot(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{\tan(\theta)} \]

Quotient Identities

Tangent
\[ \tan(\theta) = \dfrac{\sin(\theta)}{\cos(\theta)} \]
Cotangent
\[ \cot(\theta) = \dfrac{\cos(\theta)}{\sin(\theta)} \]
Why these matter
Convert everything into sine and cosine when stuck.

Pythagorean Identities

\[ \sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta) = 1 \]
\[ \sec^2(\theta) = \tan^2(\theta) + 1 \]
\[ \csc^2(\theta) = \cot^2(\theta) + 1 \]

Example 4a: Simplify \( \sec(\theta)\cos(\theta) \)

Try this yourself first.

Step 1: Replace \( \sec(\theta) \) with its reciprocal form. \[ \sec(\theta)\cos(\theta) = \dfrac{1}{\cos(\theta)} \cdot \cos(\theta) \]
Step 2: Cancel. \[ = 1 \]
Answer: \( \sec(\theta)\cos(\theta) = 1 \)

Example 4b: Simplify \( \dfrac{\csc(t)}{\cot(t)} \)

Convert to sine and cosine first.

Step 1: Rewrite each function in sine/cosine form. \[ \dfrac{\csc(t)}{\cot(t)} = \dfrac{\,\tfrac{1}{\sin(t)}\,}{\,\tfrac{\cos(t)}{\sin(t)}\,} \]
Step 2: Multiply by the reciprocal of the denominator. \[ = \dfrac{1}{\sin(t)} \cdot \dfrac{\sin(t)}{\cos(t)} = \dfrac{1}{\cos(t)} \]
Step 3: Recognize the reciprocal identity. \[ = \sec(t) \]
Answer: \( \dfrac{\csc(t)}{\cot(t)} = \sec(t) \)

Example 4c: Simplify \( \dfrac{\csc^2(\theta)}{\csc^2(\theta) - 1} \)

Spot the Pythagorean identity in the denominator.

Step 1: Recall \( \csc^2(\theta) = \cot^2(\theta) + 1 \), so \( \csc^2(\theta) - 1 = \cot^2(\theta) \). \[ \dfrac{\csc^2(\theta)}{\csc^2(\theta) - 1} = \dfrac{\csc^2(\theta)}{\cot^2(\theta)} \]
Step 2: Convert both to sine/cosine. \[ = \dfrac{\,\tfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\,}{\,\tfrac{\cos^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,} = \dfrac{1}{\sin^2\theta} \cdot \dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\cos^2\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2\theta} \]
Step 3: Recognize this as \( \sec^2(\theta) \).
Answer: \( \dfrac{\csc^2(\theta)}{\csc^2(\theta) - 1} = \sec^2(\theta) \)

Example 4d: Simplify \( \dfrac{\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta)}{\sin^2(\theta)} \)

The numerator should look very familiar.

Step 1: The numerator equals 1 by the Pythagorean identity. \[ \dfrac{\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta)}{\sin^2(\theta)} = \dfrac{1}{\sin^2(\theta)} \]
Step 2: Recognize as \( \csc^2(\theta) \).
Answer: \( \dfrac{\sin^2(\theta) + \cos^2(\theta)}{\sin^2(\theta)} = \csc^2(\theta) \)

Example 4e: Verify the identity \( \sin(t)\cot(t) = \cos(t) \)

Start with the more complex side. Convert everything to sine and cosine.

Step 1: Begin with the LHS. Replace \( \cot(t) \) with \( \tfrac{\cos(t)}{\sin(t)} \). \[ \sin(t)\cot(t) = \sin(t) \cdot \dfrac{\cos(t)}{\sin(t)} \]
Step 2: Cancel the \( \sin(t) \). \[ = \cos(t) \]
Step 3: LHS = RHS. Identity verified. \( \blacksquare \)
Verified: \( \sin(t)\cot(t) = \cos(t) \)

5. Even/Odd Identities

Definitions

Sine is an odd function: \( \sin(-\theta) = -\sin(\theta) \).
Cosine is an even function: \( \cos(-\theta) = \cos(\theta) \).
(And tangent is odd: \( \tan(-\theta) = -\tan(\theta) \), since odd/even = odd.)

How to remember: Even = symmetric about the y-axis (cosine). Odd = symmetric about the origin (sine). The "minus sign" comes out of sine but disappears for cosine.

Example 5: Rewrite \( \cos(-t) = \sin(-t) \) to eliminate the negative signs.

Apply each even/odd identity.

Step 1: Cosine is even — \( \cos(-t) = \cos(t) \).
Step 2: Sine is odd — \( \sin(-t) = -\sin(t) \).
Step 3: Substitute back into the equation. \[ \cos(t) = -\sin(t) \]
Answer: \( \cos(t) = -\sin(t) \)

6. Double-Angle, Half-Angle & Sum/Difference Identities

Double-Angle Identities

Sine
\[ \sin(2a) = 2\sin(a)\cos(a) \]
Cosine (3 forms)
\[ \cos(2a) = \cos^2(a) - \sin^2(a) \] \[ = 1 - 2\sin^2(a) \] \[ = 2\cos^2(a) - 1 \]
Tangent
\[ \tan(2a) = \dfrac{2\tan(a)}{1 - \tan^2(a)} \]

Sum & Difference Identities

\[ \sin(\alpha \pm \beta) = \sin\alpha \cos\beta \pm \cos\alpha \sin\beta \]
\[ \cos(\alpha \pm \beta) = \cos\alpha \cos\beta \mp \sin\alpha \sin\beta \]
\[ \tan(\alpha \pm \beta) = \dfrac{\tan\alpha \pm \tan\beta}{1 \mp \tan\alpha \tan\beta} \]

Half-Angle Identities

\[ \sin\!\left(\tfrac{a}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{1 - \cos a}{2}} \]
\[ \cos\!\left(\tfrac{a}{2}\right) = \pm\sqrt{\tfrac{1 + \cos a}{2}} \]
\[ \tan\!\left(\tfrac{a}{2}\right) = \dfrac{1 - \cos a}{\sin a} \]
Sign choice for half-angle: The \( \pm \) is decided by which quadrant \( \tfrac{a}{2} \) lies in.

Example 6: If \( \cos(x) = \tfrac{8}{19} \), find \( \sin(2x) \).

You'll need both Pythagorean and double-angle identities.

Step 1: We need \( \sin(x) \). Use the Pythagorean identity. \[ \sin^2(x) = 1 - \cos^2(x) = 1 - \tfrac{64}{361} = \tfrac{297}{361} \]
Step 2: Take the square root. (Without quadrant info, take the positive value.) \[ \sin(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{297}}{19} \]
Step 3: Apply the double-angle identity for sine. \[ \sin(2x) = 2\sin(x)\cos(x) = 2 \cdot \dfrac{\sqrt{297}}{19} \cdot \dfrac{8}{19} \]
Step 4: Multiply. \[ \sin(2x) = \dfrac{16\sqrt{297}}{361} \]
Answer: \( \sin(2x) = \dfrac{16\sqrt{297}}{361} \approx 0.764 \)

7. Solving Equations Using Identities

Strategy

When an equation mixes multiple trig functions or different arguments, use identities to rewrite everything in one function and one argument first. Then it becomes a basic trig equation (or a quadratic in disguise).

Example 7a: Solve \( 2\sin(\theta)\cos(\theta) = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \)

Recognize the double-angle pattern!

Step 1: The LHS is exactly \( \sin(2\theta) \). \[ \sin(2\theta) = \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \]
Step 2: Find angles where sine equals \( \tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \). \[ 2\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3} + 2\pi k \quad \text{or} \quad 2\theta = \tfrac{2\pi}{3} + 2\pi k \]
Step 3: Divide by 2. \[ \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{6} + \pi k \quad \text{or} \quad \theta = \tfrac{\pi}{3} + \pi k \]
Answer: \( \theta = \dfrac{\pi}{6} + \pi k \) or \( \theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3} + \pi k \)

Example 7b: Solve \( \sin^2(x) + \cos(2x) = 1 \)

Use a double-angle form for cosine.

Step 1: Replace \( \cos(2x) \) with \( 1 - 2\sin^2(x) \). \[ \sin^2(x) + 1 - 2\sin^2(x) = 1 \]
Step 2: Simplify. \[ -\sin^2(x) + 1 = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \sin^2(x) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \sin(x) = 0 \]
Step 3: Solve. \[ x = \pi k \]
Answer: \( x = \pi k, \quad k \in \mathbb{Z} \)

Example 7c: Solve \( \cos^2(2x) = \sin(x) \)

Hmm — this one mixes squared cosine and a single sine. Try a Pythagorean swap.

Step 1: Replace \( \cos^2(2x) = 1 - \sin^2(2x) \). \[ 1 - \sin^2(2x) = \sin(x) \]
Step 2: Now expand \( \sin(2x) = 2\sin(x)\cos(x) \), so \( \sin^2(2x) = 4\sin^2(x)\cos^2(x) \). Using \( \cos^2(x) = 1 - \sin^2(x) \): \[ 1 - 4\sin^2(x)\bigl(1 - \sin^2(x)\bigr) = \sin(x) \]
Step 3: Let \( u = \sin(x) \). Expand: \[ 1 - 4u^2 + 4u^4 = u \] \[ 4u^4 - 4u^2 - u + 1 = 0 \]
Step 4: By inspection, try \( u = \tfrac{1}{2} \): \( 4(\tfrac{1}{16}) - 4(\tfrac{1}{4}) - \tfrac{1}{2} + 1 = \tfrac{1}{4} - 1 - \tfrac{1}{2} + 1 = -\tfrac{1}{4} \neq 0 \). Try \( u = 1 \): \( 4 - 4 - 1 + 1 = 0 \). ✓
Step 5: Factor out \( (u - 1) \): \[ 4u^4 - 4u^2 - u + 1 = (u - 1)(4u^3 + 4u^2 - 1) \]
Step 6: The cubic factor \( 4u^3 + 4u^2 - 1 = 0 \) has one real root \( u \approx 0.4196 \), the others outside \( [-1, 1] \). So \( \sin(x) = 1 \) gives \( x = \tfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k \), and \( \sin(x) \approx 0.4196 \) gives \( x \approx 0.4327 + 2\pi k \) or \( x \approx \pi - 0.4327 + 2\pi k \).
Answer: \( x = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + 2\pi k \), \; \( x \approx 0.4327 + 2\pi k \), \; or \; \( x \approx 2.7089 + 2\pi k \).

Note: This problem is more involved than the others — solving the cubic factor algebraically is beyond the typical scope. Most workflows accept \( \sin(x) = 1 \) as the primary answer and graph the rest numerically.

Example 7d: Solve \( \tan^2(x) - \sec^2(x) = -1 \)

Look for a Pythagorean identity hiding in the equation.

Step 1: Recall \( \sec^2(x) = \tan^2(x) + 1 \), so \( \tan^2(x) - \sec^2(x) = \tan^2(x) - (\tan^2(x) + 1) = -1 \).
Step 2: The equation becomes \( -1 = -1 \), which is always true.
Answer: The equation is an identity — true for all \( x \) in the domain (i.e., wherever \( \cos(x) \neq 0 \)). \[ x \in \mathbb{R}, \quad x \neq \tfrac{\pi}{2} + \pi k \]

8. Collaborative Learning

👥 Work with your group. Be ready to explain your reasoning.

1. Verify or solve: \( \tan(x)\sin(x) = \cos(x) \)

Treat as an equation, not an identity. Convert \( \tan(x) \) to \( \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} \), multiply through by \( \cos(x) \), and use \( \sin^2 x + \cos^2 x = 1 \) to get a quadratic in \( \cos(x) \).

2. Solve: \( \sin^2(x) + \cos^2(x) = \sin(x) + 1 \)
Hint: Let \( u = \sin(x) \), then solve using the quadratic formula.

The LHS simplifies to 1 by the Pythagorean identity. So the equation becomes \( 1 = \sin(x) + 1 \), i.e., \( \sin(x) = 0 \).

3. Solve: \( 1 - \cos(2x) = \sin(x) \)

Use \( \cos(2x) = 1 - 2\sin^2(x) \). Then \( 1 - (1 - 2\sin^2 x) = 2\sin^2 x = \sin x \). Factor: \( \sin(x)(2\sin(x) - 1) = 0 \).

4. Solve: \( \sin(2x) = \cos(x) \)

Replace \( \sin(2x) = 2\sin(x)\cos(x) \). Then \( 2\sin(x)\cos(x) - \cos(x) = 0 \). Factor: \( \cos(x)\bigl(2\sin(x) - 1\bigr) = 0 \).

Q1. Which identity is most useful for solving \( 2\sin(x)\cos(x) = \tfrac{1}{2} \)?

Q2. True or False: \( \cos(-\theta) = -\cos(\theta) \).

Q3. Briefly explain: when you solve \( \sin(Bx) = c \), why do you need to add \( \tfrac{2\pi k}{B} \) instead of \( 2\pi k \)?


You first add \( 2\pi k \) to the inside expression \( Bx \), since that's the period of sine in terms of the inside argument. When you divide by \( B \) to isolate \( x \), the \( 2\pi k \) term gets divided too — giving \( \tfrac{2\pi k}{B} \). The graph of \( \sin(Bx) \) completes \( B \) cycles in the same horizontal distance, so the period in \( x \) is \( \tfrac{2\pi}{B} \).
